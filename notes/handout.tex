\begin{abstract}
How much longitudinal antibody data must a vaccine trial accumulate before it can make a credible
statement about the duration of protection? At a planned review, this becomes a practical design
question: is the evidence sufficient for durability follow-up to conclude, or is further observation
still likely to change the answer? This workshop project will approach that question through
simulation and model-based extrapolation. The working application is COVID-19 vaccination, for
which both antibody-kinetics models and quantitative links between neutralisation and protection
are available. The choices below are starting points for discussion, not a fixed analysis protocol.
\end{abstract}

\section{Project question and aims}\label{sec:aims}

Consider a vaccine trial, or a long-term extension, that measures an antibody marker repeatedly
after vaccination. Antibody levels often fall quickly at first and more slowly later. We would like
to estimate when the marker will fall below a protection-relevant level, even when that crossing
occurs beyond the observed follow-up. Immune correlates require careful terminology---a correlate
can predict protection without being its sole cause---but they can still support useful quantitative
predictions \parencite{plotkin2012,earle2021}.

The central question is therefore:

\begin{quote}
\emph{At a planned trial review, how long must participants have been followed, and how informative
must their longitudinal measurements be, before the duration of antibody-associated protection can
be estimated credibly enough to decide whether further durability follow-up is needed?}
\end{quote}

The project has four linked aims:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item describe plausible short- and long-term antibody trajectories;
  \item quantify how much extrapolation is being asked of a fitted model;
  \item understand when apparently similar fits imply very different durations of protection;
  \item translate those findings into practical guidance on the duration and timing of trial
        follow-up.
\end{enumerate}

The distinction between concluding durability follow-up and stopping all trial activity is
important: safety surveillance, clinical endpoints and regulatory commitments may independently
require continued follow-up. The present project concerns the information available for the
durability question.

This decision problem is clearest when protection is expected to last for years. The pivotal
Shingrix trials followed participants for roughly three to four years, while their long-term
extension ultimately assessed efficacy and immunogenicity through 11 years after vaccination
\parencite{strezova2025}. Such studies show the value of long follow-up, but also motivate asking at
which earlier review points the accumulated marker data can already support a reliable conclusion,
and when additional observations are still needed to resolve the tail.

COVID-19 provides a well-developed starting point: neutralisation is predictive of protection,
and published work connects titres, variants, boosting and vaccine effectiveness
\parencite{khoury2021,cromer2022,gilbert2022,feng2021}. Whether the same framework transfers to
other pathogens, assays and types of immune marker is deliberately left open for the workshop.

\section{What do we mean by duration of protection?}\label{sec:estimand}

Let $c_i(t)$ denote the latent antibody concentration for participant $i$ at time $t$, and let
$c_{\mathrm{thr}}$ be a protection-relevant threshold on the same assay scale. A natural
individual-level target is the first threshold-crossing time
\begin{equation}
  T_i^* = \inf\{t\geq 0 : c_i(t)\leq c_{\mathrm{thr}}\}.
  \label{eq:tstar}
\end{equation}
This quantity is a model-based estimand, not a directly observed biological expiry date. It can
be undefined if the trajectory approaches a plateau above the threshold, and it depends on the
endpoint and threshold definition.

For the initial COVID-19 application, let $c_{\mathrm{conv}}$ denote the mean convalescent
neutralisation level and express both $c_i(t)$ and $c_{\mathrm{thr}}$ relative to that reference.
One option is then to anchor $c_{\mathrm{thr}}$ to the level associated with 50\% protection in the
logistic relationship estimated by \textcite{khoury2021}. The same fitted antibody curve could
instead be passed through the full titre-to-protection relationship to obtain a protection curve
$E_i(t)$. This makes explicit that ``antibody duration'' and ``protection duration'' are related but
not interchangeable.

Heterogeneity creates a second distinction. The distribution of individual $T_i^*$ values is not
equivalent to the threshold-crossing time of a population-average antibody curve. A population
target might instead be the time at which the fraction of people still above the threshold falls
below a chosen level. The workshop should keep individual and population questions separate.

\section{Candidate trajectory models}\label{sec:models}

The candidate set is intended to expose different assumptions about the unobserved tail. We
suggest the biphasic exponential as a common reference model for the first analyses, while
retaining the other families as substantive alternatives.

\subsection*{Single exponential}
\begin{equation}
  c(t)=c_0e^{-kt}.
\end{equation}
This is the simplest reference model. The log concentration has a constant slope and
$T^*=k^{-1}\log(c_0/c_{\mathrm{thr}})$ when $c_0>c_{\mathrm{thr}}$. It can be identified from
relatively short follow-up if the trajectory is truly exponential, but it extrapolates the early
slope indefinitely when that assumption is wrong. Single-half-life summaries are common in
analyses of COVID-19 neutralisation waning \parencite{khoury2021,cromer2022}.

\subsection*{Biphasic exponential (suggested default)}
Biphasic structures have been used successfully to describe vaccine-antibody kinetics in
established analyses, representing rapid initial contraction followed by a slower persistent
component \parencite{white2015,hogan2023}. We therefore suggest this family as the initial
reference model: its parameters have a useful biological interpretation, its long-term driver is
explicit, and a published COVID-19 calibration is available. This is a practical starting point
for comparison, rather than a claim that biphasic decay will be preferred for every dataset. We
use the smooth parameterisation of \textcite{hogan2023} from the outset:
\begin{align}
  c(t)&=c_0\{w_1e^{-k_1t}+w_2e^{-k_2t}\},
  & k_r&=\frac{\log 2}{h_r},\quad r\in\{1,2\},\quad h_1<h_2, \\
  D&=e^{-k_1t_s}+e^{-k_2t_s},
  & w_1&=\frac{e^{-k_2t_s}}{D},\qquad
    w_2=\frac{e^{-k_1t_s}}{D}.
  \label{eq:biphasic}
\end{align}
The fast component can represent contraction of short-lived antibody-secreting cells and the slow
component a longer-lived population. The weights ensure $c(0)=c_0$ and make the two weighted
components equal at switching time $t_s$. The slow half-life $h_2$ governs long-term duration but
may be weakly identified while the fast component dominates.

\subsection*{Power-law decay}
\begin{equation}
  c(t)=c_0(1+\alpha t)^{-\beta}.
\end{equation}
This curve decelerates continuously. It can resemble an exponential over a short interval while
implying a much longer tail. Long-term antibody data provide biological motivation for considering
decelerating decay rather than a constant half-life \parencite{amanna2007}.

\subsection*{Plateau or set-point}
\begin{equation}
  c(t)=A_\infty +(c_0-A_\infty)e^{-kt}.
\end{equation}
Here the long-term level is non-zero. A crossing exists only when
$A_\infty<c_{\mathrm{thr}}$. Short data may not distinguish a genuine plateau from a very slow
decline, making ``never crosses'' an informative possibility rather than a numerical failure.
Long-term measurements showing durable antibody levels motivate this alternative
\parencite{amanna2007}.

\subsection*{Mechanistic antibody-production model}
A richer generating model can represent short- and long-lived antibody-secreting cells and
antibody clearance separately:
\begin{align}
  \frac{dP_s}{dt}&=-\delta_sP_s, &
  \frac{dP_l}{dt}&=-\delta_lP_l, &
  \frac{dc}{dt}&=\rho_sP_s+\rho_lP_l-\delta_cc.
\end{align}
When antibody clears quickly relative to the cells, this approaches biphasic decay; added memory
or replenishment can generate an even slower tail. Related models have been developed for plasma
cell dynamics and vaccine responses \parencite{andraud2012,pasin2019}. Such a model may be most
useful as a deliberately richer data-generating mechanism rather than as the default fitted model.

A complementary later-stage generator could use the semi-mechanistic model developed across a
series of COVID-19 studies \parencite{nishiyama2023,nakamura2024,park2025,hart2025}. It links a
decaying mRNA stimulus to delayed, nonlinear antibody production and antibody loss, and can
represent boosting, infection history and between-participant variation. Public code and derived
parameter estimates make it a practical calibration resource. Once vaccine stimulation has
vanished, however, its long-term tail tends towards a single exponential, so it is better suited to
testing dose-response dynamics and heterogeneous trajectories than to replacing the richer
slow-tail generator above.

\section{Observation model and biphasic population parameterisation}\label{sec:parameterisation}

Observed assays are naturally treated as noisy measurements of a latent positive curve. A simple
starting point is multiplicative log-normal error,
\begin{equation}
  \log y_{ij}=\log c_i(t_{ij})+\epsilon_{ij},
  \qquad \epsilon_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma_{\log}^2).
  \label{eq:observation}
\end{equation}
Lower and upper quantification limits can be represented as censoring rather than replacing
censored observations by exact boundary values. This observation model can be used with any of
the candidate trajectories in Section~\ref{sec:models}.

For the suggested biphasic reference model, between-participant variation can be introduced
through correlated log-normal random effects on peak level and the slow decay rate, for example
\begin{align}
  c_{0i}&=c_0e^{\eta_{0i}}, & k_{2i}&=k_2e^{\eta_{ki}}, \\
  \begin{pmatrix}\eta_{0i}\\ \eta_{ki}\end{pmatrix}
  &\sim N_2\!\left[
    \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix},
    \begin{pmatrix}
      \omega_0^2 & \rho_{0k}\omega_0\omega_k \\
      \rho_{0k}\omega_0\omega_k & \omega_k^2
    \end{pmatrix}\right].
  \label{eq:heterogeneity}
\end{align}
This permits both individual and population targets and asks whether additional participants
compensate for sparse information within each trajectory. Population mechanistic models have used
this type of structure in vaccine applications \parencite{pasin2019}.

\section{Proposed priors with a common early decline}\label{sec:priors}

For the initial homogeneous analysis, we propose matching prior information
about the early antibody trajectory across candidate families. Their
long-term predictions may differ: an exponential can estimate early decline
precisely yet confidently misestimate a much later threshold crossing. The
priors below are workshop modelling choices centred on the calibration in
Table~\ref{tab:starting-values}, not published estimates of prior uncertainty.
They specify fitting assumptions separately from the generating truth.

Fit each family once at each review, then calculate both crossing times from
every posterior trajectory draw, using $c_{\mathrm{sym}}=0.202$ and
$c_{\mathrm{sev}}=0.030$. This preserves their joint uncertainty and gives
$T^*_{\mathrm{sym}}\leq T^*_{\mathrm{sev}}$. Keep the selected prior
specification fixed across review dates, cohort sizes and truth scenarios;
do not recalculate its centres from each simulated dataset. The thresholds
are fixed protection anchors for this initial comparison, with their
uncertainty reserved for a separate sensitivity analysis.

\subsection*{Common early-trajectory parameters}

Let the reference day be $t_a=90$ and define the positive cumulative log
decline and the fraction remaining by
\begin{equation}
  D_a=-\log\{c(t_a)/c_0\}>0,\qquad R_a=e^{-D_a}.
  \label{eq:prior-anchor}
\end{equation}
Every family is parameterised so that $c(0)=c_0$ and $c(t_a)=c_0R_a$.
Consequently, the joint prior for these two titres is identical across
families. Curvature between these times remains model-specific. The
reference day stays at 90 days even when a review occurs earlier or later;
it defines a parameter, not an additional observation.

\subsection*{Consistent model parameterisations}

The following transformations introduce the additional free variables and
ensure that every family has the common initial titre and day-90 decline.
All logarithms are natural, and all times are in days.

\paragraph{Single exponential.}
No additional free parameter is needed. Set
\begin{equation}
  k=D_a/t_a,\qquad c(t)=c_0e^{-kt}.
  \label{eq:prior-exponential}
\end{equation}
The model extends this early average log decline indefinitely; its late
slope is not adjusted to resemble the more flexible families.

\paragraph{Biphasic exponential.}
Introduce $u_f\in(0,1)$ and $z_q\in\mathbb{R}$. Set $f=u_f/2$,
$q=1+e^{z_q}$, and write
\begin{equation}
  c(t)=c_0\{(1-f)e^{-qst}+fe^{-st}\},\qquad s=k_2,\quad q=k_1/k_2.
  \label{eq:prior-biphasic}
\end{equation}
Here $f$ is the fraction of the initial titre contributed by the slow
component, rather than a fraction of participants. Given $D_a,f,q$, obtain
$s>0$ as the unique solution of
\begin{equation}
  (1-f)e^{-qst_a}+fe^{-st_a}=R_a.
  \label{eq:prior-biphasic-root}
\end{equation}
The left-hand side decreases continuously from 1 to 0, so a solution exists
for every allowed draw. This is the same trajectory family as
Equation~\ref{eq:biphasic}, with
\begin{equation}
  h_1=\frac{\log 2}{qs},\qquad h_2=\frac{\log 2}{s},\qquad
  t_s=\frac{\log\{(1-f)/f\}}{(q-1)s}.
  \label{eq:prior-biphasic-map}
\end{equation}
The restriction $0<f<0.5$ specifies a positive switching time and an
initially larger fast contribution. The half-lives, switching time and
slow rate are derived from $D_a,u_f,z_q$ rather than free parameters.

\paragraph{Power-law decay.}
Introduce the free exponent $\beta>0$. Given $D_a$ and $\beta$, set
\begin{equation}
  \alpha=\frac{e^{D_a/\beta}-1}{t_a},\qquad
  c(t)=c_0(1+\alpha t)^{-\beta}.
  \label{eq:prior-powerlaw}
\end{equation}
The exponent controls deceleration while $\alpha$ is determined by the
common day-90 decline and $\beta$.

\paragraph{Plateau or set-point.}
Introduce $u_\infty\in(0,1)$, the floor as a fraction of the day-90 titre,
and set
\begin{align}
  A_\infty&=u_\infty c_0R_a,\qquad r=A_\infty/c_0, \\
  k&=-\frac{1}{t_a}\log\!\left(\frac{R_a-r}{1-r}\right),\qquad
  c(t)=A_\infty+(c_0-A_\infty)e^{-kt}.
  \label{eq:prior-plateau}
\end{align}
Because $0<r<R_a<1$, the rate is positive and the floor lies below the
day-90 titre. Depending on the floor, a trajectory can cross both
thresholds, only the higher threshold, or neither.

information about every derived quantity: more diffuse priors can increase
mass on extremely long durations or initial titres below a threshold.

days. For the biphasic model, $f$ has mean 0.20 and an approximate 95\%
range of 0.034--0.403; $q$ has median 16.6 and an approximate 95\% range
of 4.3--75.8. At $D_a=1.16$, $f=0.20$ and $q=581/35$, the implied
half-lives are about 35 and 579 days, with a 74-day switching time.
For the power law, the default approximate 95\% range for $\beta$ is
0.38--5.91; the broader option gives 0.21--10.65.

For the plateau, the uniform prior on $u_\infty$ permits all three
crossing outcomes. Under the default common priors, the prior non-crossing
probabilities are approximately 0.29 for the symptomatic anchor and 0.83
for the severe anchor. These are prior implications of the floor assumption
and should be reported alongside posterior probabilities, rather than
attributed entirely to the data.

\subsection*{An alternative with common day-90 and day-365 anchors}

As a separate sensitivity formulation, match the joint prior on
$c_0,c(90),c(365)$ across the three flexible families. Retain the original
initial-level and day-90 prior for the exponential, which still extrapolates
its early decline unchanged. This supplies common information about the
first year without requiring agreement about later threshold crossings.
The anchor days define latent parameters, not additional observations;
their priors remain fixed at every review.

Set $t_b=365$, $m=t_b/t_a$, and introduce
\begin{equation}
  \rho=\frac{D_b-D_a}{(m-1)D_a}\in(0,1),\qquad
  D_b=D_a\{1+(m-1)\rho\},\qquad R_b=e^{-D_b}.
  \label{eq:two-anchor}
\end{equation}
Thus $\rho$ is the average log-decay rate from day 90 to 365 divided by
that from day 0 to 90. It enforces $R_a^m<R_b<R_a$: positive decline
with deceleration. The reference biphasic trajectory gives
$\rho\simeq0.25$. Independent priors directly on the three titres would
not enforce these constraints.

\paragraph{Power law.}
Let $v>0$ solve
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\log\{1+m(e^v-1)\}}{v}=\frac{D_b}{D_a},\qquad
  \beta=D_a/v,\qquad \alpha=(e^v-1)/t_a.
\end{equation}
The ratio decreases from $m$ to 1, so the anchors determine the two shape
parameters uniquely. Use the power-law curve above.

\paragraph{Biphasic exponential.}
Introduce one additional free variable $u_s\in(0,1)$ and set
\begin{equation}
  k_2=u_s\rho D_a/t_a,\qquad x=e^{-k_2t_a}.
\end{equation}
The slow rate is a fraction of the average day-90-to-365 log-decay rate.
Find $y\in(0,R_a)$ as the unique solution of
\begin{equation}
  f=\frac{R_a-y}{x-y},\qquad
  (1-f)y^m+fx^m=R_b,\qquad k_1=-\log(y)/t_a.
  \label{eq:two-anchor-mixture}
\end{equation}
The left-hand side decreases from $R_ax^{m-1}>R_b$ to $R_a^m<R_b$
as $y$ runs from 0 to $R_a$. Every draw therefore gives positive ordered
rates and $0<f<1$, without rejecting or reweighting the common anchors.
Use Equation~\ref{eq:prior-biphasic} with $s=k_2$ and $q=k_1/k_2$;
$f$, both half-lives and $t_s$ are derived. The reference curve has
$u_s\simeq0.37$.

For this formulation, relax the earlier restriction $f<0.5$. Some valid
anchor combinations require a larger initial slow contribution; their
equal-contribution time $t_s$ is negative. Restricting $f$ independently
and rejecting incompatible draws would change the anchor distribution.
This is an explicit difference between the two prior formulations.

\paragraph{Plateau.}
Use Equation~\ref{eq:two-anchor-mixture} with $x=1$, representing a
zero-rate slow component. Set $A_\infty=c_0f$ and $k=-\log(y)/t_a$ in
the plateau curve. Both shape parameters are then determined by the
anchors. A positive floor still permits infinite crossing times.

All free variables in this formulation are independent. The two priors on
$\rho$ have approximate 95\% ranges 0.045--0.70 and 0.017--0.86;
both priors on $u_s$ have mean 0.40. These are workshop choices, not
external estimates. Neither the earlier $f,q$ priors nor the earlier
$\beta,u_\infty$ priors are also imposed. In particular, the plateau's
non-crossing probabilities must be recomputed under these priors.

Inspect prior trajectories and both crossing distributions before fitting,
including the implied distribution of $f$ and probability $f>0.5$.
Agreement at three anchors does not imply agreement between them or in
the tail. Matching at day 365 also supplies prior information about an
unobserved period at early reviews, so compare this formulation with the
original early-only formulation rather than treating it as neutral.

\section{A possible analysis programme}\label{sec:analysis}

A simulation-first programme would create a full longitudinal dataset from a known trajectory,
then reveal the portions available at successive planned trial reviews. Each candidate model could
be fitted to the data available at each review and extrapolated to $T^*$. Because the generating
truth is known, the exercise can separate uncertainty from confident misspecification and ask when
a decision to conclude durability follow-up would have been well supported.

A natural progression is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item establish a transparent homogeneous baseline in which a biphasic model is fitted to
        biphasic data;
  \item reveal successively longer follow-up windows and examine when conclusions about duration
        become stable, or only appear stable;
  \item fit all candidate families to the same truncated data to expose disagreement in the tail;
  \item generate data from richer mechanistic models and ask whether simple fitted models remain
        useful;
  \item introduce participant heterogeneity and compare individual with population duration;
  \item if useful, map antibody trajectories to symptomatic and severe-disease protection or
        compare the framework with an open real dataset.
\end{enumerate}

Points for discussion include which duration is clinically meaningful; how strongly to privilege
COVID-19; which model families and parameterisations are worth pursuing; how inference and study
design should be handled; what evidence should be sufficient to conclude durability follow-up;
which comparisons would be most informative; and what degree of extrapolation would be acceptable
to a trial designer.

\subsection*{A single-participant computational pilot}

An initial illustration of the first three steps uses one homogeneous
participant and one simulated history from Table~\ref{tab:starting-values}.
Monthly measurements include day zero and visits at rounded days
$365.25j/12$, revealed at the configured 3, 6, 9, 12, 18 and 24 month
reviews. Both prior columns in Table~\ref{tab:priors} are fitted to exactly
the same measurements. Repeat with both columns of
Table~\ref{tab:two-anchor-priors}, using exactly the same simulated history,
to separate differences due to the prior formulation from observation
noise. This single realisation describes individual
posterior uncertainty and misspecification; it cannot estimate empirical
coverage or identify an adequate trial design.

Use four independent JAGS chains, initially with 2000 adaptation iterations,
2000 further warm-up iterations and 4000 retained draws per chain. Require
rank-normalised split $\widehat R<1.01$ and bulk and tail effective sample
sizes of at least 400 for the free parameters and duration-relevant
functions. For a potentially infinite duration, diagnose the bounded
transform $T^*/(365.25+T^*)$, defined as 1 at infinity, and separately
check a non-constant non-crossing indicator. If a duration quantile falls
on an atom so that its quantile indicator is constant, its tail effective
sample size is not applicable; use the non-constant quantile indicators
and the bulk diagnostic. Constant indicators are reported as such rather
than assigned a fictitious effective sample size. Extend
sampling if needed, recording every attempt and unresolved failure.

Compare posterior trajectories and both duration distributions. As a
numerical check, calculate extreme power-law prior durations on the log
scale before taking quantiles. A finite duration beyond floating-point
range is not a non-crossing trajectory; record a numerical failure if it
prevents a fitted result from meeting the shared output contract, retaining
the sampler output for inspection. As a
secondary descriptive comparison, report pointwise PSIS leave-one-out
expected log predictive density for the observed log titres, with its
Pareto-$k$ diagnostics; values above 0.7 flag an unreliable approximation.
This comparison concerns interpolation among observed visits and does not
validate long-term extrapolation. Also evaluate predictions for the
subsequent observed visits withheld at each review, clearly separating this
simulation check from information available to the fit. Inspect prior
predictive trajectories under both prior sets before interpreting results.

\subsection*{Repeated single-participant estimation study}

For the first repeated-simulation comparison, use the early-only default
column of Table~\ref{tab:priors}. Generate $R=100$ independent noisy
histories of one participant each, holding the biphasic generating
parameters, monthly visits, noise and six reviews fixed as above. Each
history is shared across models and nested reviews. Fit all four families
with the same sampler and convergence rules, giving 2400 planned fits.
This assesses repeated-measurement uncertainty at one fixed truth, not
between-participant variation or prior-predictive calibration. Derive every
simulation and chain seed from the design and replicate.

For endpoint $e$ and successful replicate $r$, let $m_{re}$ be the posterior
median and $[L_{re},U_{re}]$ its central 90\% interval. Report
\begin{equation}
  \widehat C_e=\frac1{R_{\mathrm{ok}}}\sum_r
    \mathbf1\{L_{re}\leq T_e^*\leq U_{re}\},\qquad
  \widehat B_e=\frac1{R_{\mathrm{ok}}}\sum_r(m_{re}-T_e^*).
\end{equation}
Keep infinite medians and interval endpoints. Summarise precision by the
median interval width, taking an interval with either endpoint infinite
to be unbounded for this summary, and separately report the unbounded
interval fraction, infinite-median fraction and mean posterior non-crossing
probability. Do not report finite-only bias as unconditional bias.

For a finite observed truth $y$, CRPS has the CDF representation
\parencite{gneiting2007}
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{CRPS}(F,y)=\int_0^\infty
    \{F(t)-\mathbf1(y\leq t)\}^2\,dt.
\end{equation}
It diverges when $F$ has positive non-crossing mass, and may also diverge
for heavy finite tails. The primary comparable score is therefore CRPS
for $Z=g(T^*)$, where $g(t)=t/(\tau+t)$, $g(\infty)=1$ and
$\tau=365.25$ days. This is a one-to-one bounded transformation, so its
CRPS scores the full extended duration distribution. Also report the
restricted day-scale score
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{CRPS}_{H}(F,y)=\int_0^H
    \{F(t)-\mathbf1(y\leq t)\}^2\,dt,
  \qquad H=3652.5\text{ days},
\end{equation}
equivalently CRPS of $\min(T^*,H)$ against $\min(y,H)$. Check horizons
1826.25 and 7305 days as sensitivities. These caps apply only to the score,
not to posterior crossing times. Lower scores are better. Compute empirical
CDF integrals exactly between sorted draws, avoiding cancellation of large
terms in the pairwise-distance formula. Retain empirical unrestricted
day-scale CRPS as a tail diagnostic; a finite Monte Carlo value does not
establish finiteness of the underlying score.

Also report mean bias and mean interval width on the bounded $g$ scale,
which remain defined when raw duration summaries are infinite. Give Monte
Carlo standard errors for finite means and Wilson 95\% intervals for
coverage and proportions. Report planned, completed and converged counts
for every model/review; primary metric summaries use converged fits only.
Bound all-planned coverage by assigning every unsuccessful fit first a
coverage failure and then a success. Retain unresolved fits and numerical
failures in the outputs. Pointwise LOO is not needed for this study.

\section{How the literature informs the project}\label{sec:literature-guide}

\textbf{Plotkin and Gilbert (2012).} This short framework gives us the vocabulary needed to
distinguish a statistical correlate, a mechanistic correlate and a surrogate endpoint, helping us
avoid interpreting a protection-relevant antibody marker as the whole immune mechanism
\parencite{plotkin2012}.

\textbf{Khoury et al. (2021).} This is the main quantitative bridge from neutralisation titre to
protection: it supplies the logistic relationship and relative 50\%-protection anchors that can be
used to turn a fitted antibody curve into an endpoint-specific protection curve
\parencite{khoury2021}.

\textbf{Cromer et al. (2022).} The meta-analysis extends the titre-to-protection framework across
variants and boosting, reminding us that starting titre and antigenic change can alter protection
duration even when the underlying decay process is unchanged \parencite{cromer2022}.

\textbf{Gilbert et al. (2022).} The mRNA-1273 efficacy-trial analysis provides trial-level evidence
that neutralisation is a graded correlate of risk, supporting continuous marker models rather than
only a binary laboratory cut-off \parencite{gilbert2022}.

\textbf{Feng et al. (2021).} This analysis of ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 similarly supports a continuous
relationship between immune markers and symptomatic or asymptomatic infection, and cautions
against treating any single threshold as universal \parencite{feng2021}.

\textbf{Earle et al. (2021).} The cross-vaccine synthesis supplies broader evidence that antibody
levels can function as a protective correlate for COVID-19 vaccines, strengthening the case for
the marker-based starting point while not establishing causality by itself \parencite{earle2021}.

\textbf{Hogan et al. (2023).} This model-based study provides vaccine- and dose-specific initial
immunity levels and the tentative COVID-19 biphasic calibration---including fast and slow
half-lives and a switching time---and illustrates how immune kinetics can be connected to
longer-term effectiveness against variants \parencite{hogan2023}.

\textbf{Nishiyama et al. (2023).} This study extends an mRNA-stimulus antibody model to a booster
dose and infection history, showing how repeated immune exposures can alter response duration and
providing a compact semi-mechanistic alternative for later simulations \parencite{nishiyama2023}.

\textbf{Nakamura et al. (2024).} Dense measurements from a small calibration cohort and sparse
measurements from more than 2,400 participants are combined to reconstruct heterogeneous
individual trajectories after primary vaccination. The fitted parameter distributions are a useful
COVID-19 calibration source, although the underlying clinical dataset is access-restricted
\parencite{nakamura2024}.

\textbf{Park et al. (2025).} Longitudinal trajectories after primary and booster vaccination are
stratified into durable, vulnerable and rapid-declining response patterns, with rapid decline linked
to earlier subsequent infection. This makes heterogeneity clinically relevant rather than merely a
statistical complication \parencite{park2025}.

\textbf{Hart et al. (2025).} The same individual-level antibody model is linked to susceptibility
and population outbreak risk. Its open code and derived parameter estimates provide an immediately
usable resource for simulation and calibration \parencite{hart2025}.

\textbf{White et al. (2015).} The RTS,S malaria analysis is the closest published analogue to the
project's intended chain: it uses biphasic antibody kinetics and links the resulting waning marker
to duration of vaccine efficacy \parencite{white2015}.

\textbf{Strezova et al. (2025).} The final Shingrix long-term follow-up analysis demonstrates the
practical scale of the design problem: an extension of pivotal phase 3 trials followed more than
7,000 participants and assessed protection through 11 years. It provides a concrete setting in
which the value of additional durability data must be weighed against prolonged follow-up
\parencite{strezova2025}.

\textbf{Andraud et al. (2012).} The nested plasma-cell and antibody models provide a clear
mechanistic template for generating trajectories on several biological time scales and for asking
when a simpler decay curve is an adequate approximation \parencite{andraud2012}.

\textbf{Pasin et al. (2019).} The Ebola prime-boost study demonstrates how mechanistic antibody
dynamics and between-participant variation can be estimated together, making it a useful model for
the hierarchical phase of this project \parencite{pasin2019}.

\textbf{Amanna et al. (2007).} Long-term measurements across antigens show that antibody durability
varies greatly and need not follow a single constant half-life, motivating the power-law and
plateau alternatives and explicit attention to the tail \parencite{amanna2007}.
